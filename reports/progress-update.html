Trace-Conditioned Test Generation

Amogh Shetty (avs97) and Ishaan Shah (ics35) · November 23, 2026 · Update to the October 19 midterm report, which follows

01How far along we are

At the midterm we had a working pipeline and two pilots on 20 tasks. Since then we have run the full study. Of the 169 tasks we selected, 148 now run end to end, each with one patch the benchmark marks as unresolved (a wrong patch) and one it marks as resolved (a correct patch). We ran all six conditions on these tasks with two tester models: gpt-5.6-terra through Azure OpenAI (147 tasks) and Kimi-K3.1-Pro, an open-weight model we also reach through Azure (148 tasks). That is roughly 3,500 graded runs for one seed, against 480 at the midterm. We also tried four other testers on small pilots, and we answered a question raised on the midterm about which part of the trace led to the test that caught the bug.

To make the setup clear: the tester is not a tool-using agent. It gets a fixed prompt with the issue, the agent's patch, the changed files and the condition's extra context, writes one pytest file, and sees the failures and patch coverage for up to three rounds. The traces come from Qwen3-Coder-480B running in OpenHands (the published SWE-rebench trajectories). A test flags a patch when it fails on the agent's patch and passes on the human fix. A flag on a wrong patch is a catch, and a flag on a correct patch is a false alarm.

Kimi catches more wrong patches than Terra in every condition (42.7% against 30.6% overall), but it also raises more false alarms (11.0% against 6.2%). Both testers catch more with a trace than without one. On all 148 tasks, Kimi flags 69 wrong patches with the trace summary (C3) and 69 with the raw trace (C5), against 58 for C2. Paired by task, C3 catches 20 patches that C2 misses and misses 9 that C2 catches (exact paired test, p = 0.061); for C5 the counts are 22 and 11 (p = 0.080). The direction matches the pilot, but with one seed it is not yet significant.

The mismatched control X is where the two testers differ, and we think this is the main result of this update. For Terra, the matching raw trace beats a trace from another task by about 11 points (37.0% against 26.2%) and also gives its fewest false alarms. On all 147 of Terra's tasks the picture is the same (C5 at 36.3%, X at 25.3%). For Kimi, the wrong trace helps almost as much as the right one (42.9% against 46.5%). This surprised us. X uses the closest-length trace from another task, which for 9 tasks came from the same project, but leaving those out barely changes it, and 49 of Kimi's 56 X catches are bugs that C1 or C2 also catch. Kimi seems to do better with any long agent log in front of it, so whether the trace content matters may depend on the tester.

Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite caught 13.2% of wrong patches on 19 tasks, and DeepSeek-V4-Flash caught 21.7% on 20 tasks with almost no false alarms (0.8%). gpt-oss-120b, served with vLLM on a Perlmutter A100 node, caught none of 7 hard bugs that Terra caught in a gate test, so we stopped it. A gpt-5.6-sol run has finished only 2 of 30 tasks.

03What in the trace helped

On the midterm we were asked what in the trace actually helped the tester. We answered this for Kimi using every strict trace-only catch: a wrong patch flagged in C3, C4 or C5 but not in C1, C2 or X. There are 13 such tasks covering 18 runs. For each flagged test we looked for the part of the exact prompt Kimi received that the test relies on. In 16 of the 18 runs the test links to a specific span of the trace, and none came from a dead end or a reverted edit.

We tagged the runs with the help of an LLM and checked 20 of the 29 tagged runs by hand, including every one marked low or medium confidence. All 20 held up. The links rest on matching content, since Kimi's own reasoning quotes the span directly in only 15 of 39 linked tests. Terra's catches are not tagged yet.
